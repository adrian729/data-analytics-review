# Analytical Thinking

Identifying and defining problems, then solving them using data in an organized, step-by-step manner.

## Analytical Skills

**Analytical skills** are qualities and characteristics that help you use facts to solve problems.

- **Curiosity:** The desire to learn, ask questions, and explore.
- **Understanding context:** Understanding the conditions in which something exists or happens. Consider the who, what, and why behind the data to interpret its meaning and recognize relevant connections.
- **Technical mindset:** Breaking down problems, tasks, or processes into smaller, logical steps.
- **Data design:** Organizing information or data so that it makes sense and is accessible and useful.
- **Data strategy:** Managing the people, processes, and tools involved in data analysis: who does what, which methods are used, and which tools are needed.

## Key Aspects of Analytical Thinking

- **Visualization:** Use [[Communicating Results#Data Visualization|data visualization]] to represent information graphically.
- **Strategy:** Defining what we want to achieve and how to get there. This helps guide efforts to improve data quality and usefulness.
- **Problem orientation:** Identifying, describing, and solving problems.
- **Correlation:** A statistical association between variables. Establishing a cause-and-effect relationship requires additional evidence.
- **Big-picture and detail-oriented thinking:** Understanding the overall situation as well as its individual parts. Keep the goal in view, identify which details matter, and focus time and resources on them.

## Common Problem Types

- **Making predictions:** Using data to make informed estimates about what may happen in the future.
- **Categorizing things:** Assigning data to groups based on shared features.
- **Spotting something unusual:** Identifying data that differs from the norm.
- **Identifying themes:** Grouping categories into broader concepts.
- **Discovering connections:** Finding similar challenges across different entities and using shared data or insights to identify common solutions.
- **Finding patterns:** Using historical data to understand recurring events or relationships and assess how likely they are to occur again.

## Asking Effective Questions: SMART

The **SMART methodology** evaluates questions by whether they are specific, measurable, action-oriented, relevant, and time-bound. Use it to ask questions that help define and solve a problem.

- **Specific:** Keep the question clear, meaningful, and focused on one topic or a few closely related ideas.
  - Broad: “Do you exercise?”
  - More specific: “On how many days last week did you do at least 60 minutes of physical activity?”
- **Measurable:** Seek answers that can be quantified and assessed.
  - Example: “How many times did this issue occur last month?”
- **Action-oriented:** Seek information that can guide a plan or encourage change.
  - Example: “What design features would make our product easier for users to recycle?”
- **Relevant:** Address something that matters to the problem being solved.
- **Time-bound:** Specify the period being studied.

### Questions to Avoid

- **Leading question:** Pushes the respondent toward a particular answer.
  - Example: “This tool is easy to use, isn't it?”
- **Unfair question:** Contains assumptions or makes it difficult to answer honestly.
  - Example: “What do you love about this tool?” assumes the respondent loves it.

### Topics to Clarify

- **Objectives:** What are the goals and expectations?
- **Audience:** Who are the stakeholders and users, and what do they need?
- **Time:** What period should the analysis cover, and when must it be completed?
- **Resources:** What people, tools, data, and other resources are available?
- **Security:** Who should have access, and what information needs protection?

## Problem-Solving Methods

### Structured Thinking

**Structured thinking** means clarifying the current problem, organizing the available information, finding gaps and opportunities, and identifying possible options.

### Reframing

**Reframing** means restating a problem or challenge and redirecting it toward a possible resolution. It can help turn an unclear or unrealistic request into an achievable next step.

### Gap Analysis

A method for comparing how a process currently works with how you want it to work in the future, and identifying the changes needed to close that gap.

### Root Cause Analysis: The Five Whys

A **root cause** is the underlying reason a problem occurs.

The **Five Whys** is a method of repeatedly asking why a problem occurs, with each answer guiding the next question, to work toward its root cause.

## Algorithms

An **algorithm** is an ordered procedure or set of rules for completing a particular task.
