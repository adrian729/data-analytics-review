# Data Collection and Quality

## Collecting and Selecting Data

### Collection Methods

- **Interviews:** Ask people questions directly.
- **Observations:** Record behavior, events, or conditions.
- **Forms, questionnaires, and surveys:** Gather responses to questions, including fixed choices or open-ended answers.
- **Cookies:** Small pieces of data stored by websites in a browser; some are used to collect information about website use.

### Data Sources

**By original collection:**

- **Primary:** Collected first-hand for the current research question, such as interviews conducted for the study.
- **Secondary:** Existing data reused for the current study, such as published census data, previous research, or earlier organizational records.

**By origin relative to the organization:**

- **Internal:** Generated within the organization's own activities or systems, such as sales records.
- **External:** Generated by sources outside the organization, such as national statistics.

**By provider relationship:**

- **First-party:** Collected directly by you or your organization.
- **Second-party:** Another organization's first-party data, obtained directly from that organization.
- **Third-party:** Obtained through a provider that did not originally collect it, often combining several sources.

Check accuracy, bias, credibility, and suitability regardless of the source.

### Evaluating Data Sources

Use **ROCCC** to assess a source, alongside its relevance to the question.

| Criterion | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Reliable** | Documented methods, accuracy, and potential bias. |
| **Original** | Traceability to the original collector or publication. |
| **Comprehensive** | Coverage of the people, variables, and periods needed for the task. |
| **Current** | Whether the data's age and update frequency suit the question. |
| **Cited** | Identifiable creators, collection dates, and supporting documentation. |

### Open Data Resources

See [[Data Resources]] for dataset catalogs and topic-specific sources. Check reuse terms and access or computing costs for each dataset.

### Population and Sample

- **Population:** The complete group of people, items, or observations you want to study.
- **Sample:** A subset of the population used for analysis.
- **Representative sample:** A sample that reflects the population's characteristics relevant to the question.
- **Unbiased sampling:** A selection process that avoids systematically favoring or excluding parts of the target population.

Choose the sample size and selection method to fit the question. Random sampling may suit some projects; others require a more targeted approach.

### Selecting the Right Data

- **Relevance:** Choose data that helps answer the business question.
- **Type:** Match the data to the analysis; studying trends needs observations with dates.
- **Amount:** Collect enough relevant data to support the intended conclusions.
- **Time frame:** Decide which period the data must cover and how long collection will take. If an answer is needed immediately, existing historical data may be more practical than new collection.

## Bias in Data

- **Bias:** A conscious or unconscious preference for or against a person, group, or thing.
- **Data bias:** Systematic error in how data is collected, measured, processed, or interpreted that skews results in a particular direction.

### Common Types

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sampling bias** | Selection or participation systematically overrepresents or underrepresents parts of the target population. |
| **Observer bias** | An observer's expectations, beliefs, or habits systematically influence what they notice, measure, or record. Observer-expectancy bias is one form; observer bias falls within broader experimenter or researcher bias. |
| **Interpretation bias** | Ambiguous information is interpreted through existing assumptions or a tendency toward positive or negative explanations. |
| **Confirmation bias** | Information is sought, selected, or interpreted to support existing beliefs. |

### Reducing Bias

- Use appropriate random sampling and check who is covered, selected, and actually participates.
- Compare the sample's relevant characteristics with the target population.
- Use clear questions and consistent measurement procedures.
- Consider alternative explanations and actively examine evidence that challenges the initial expectation.

## Data Quality and Limitations

- **Incomplete or missing data:** Gaps may prevent the analysis from answering the intended question. Explain what is missing and how it limits the conclusions.
- **Misaligned data:** Sources may use different definitions, metrics, units, or periods. Check that they measure comparable things before combining or comparing them.
- **Dirty data:** Errors, duplicates, and inconsistent formatting can produce misleading results; identify and correct them.
- **Sample size:** Small samples can produce unstable results. Consider the amount of data and how well it represents the population before generalizing findings.

See [[Communicating Results#Presenting Findings Accurately|Presenting Findings Accurately]] for communicating comparisons and uncertainty.

## Data Transformation

**Data transformation** changes data's format, structure, or values to make it suitable for a task.

Examples include standardizing field names or units, converting data types, combining datasets, reshaping wide and long layouts, and exporting to another file format.

Choose transformations that support compatibility, analysis, or meaningful comparisons. When reshaping, preserve the intended level of detail and check that identifiers and values remain correctly matched.

## Fairness and Data Collection

**Fairness** means conducting analysis in ways that avoid creating or reinforcing bias.

- **Oversampling:** Increasing the sample size of underrepresented groups to improve their representation in a dataset.
- **Self-reporting:** Collecting data by asking participants to provide information about themselves.

See [[Data Ethics and Privacy]] for principles governing personal data and its use.
