**[Bias](/glossary#bias)** is a conscious or subconscious preference in favor of or against a person, group, or thing. Brains are built to make fast judgments, so bias shows up constantly in everyday life — and it doesn't stay out of data just because the data is numeric.

## Data bias

**[Data bias](/glossary#data-bias)** is what happens when that kind of preference systematically skews an analysis in a particular direction. It can enter at any stage: a survey's questions can be slanted to nudge answers, a sample group can leave out people it should include, or a rushed data-collection window can push respondents toward careless, lower-quality answers. The impact is not abstract — clinical heart-health studies have historically included far more men than women, contributing to women's symptoms going unrecognized and untreated.

## Named varieties

A few patterns come up often enough to have their own names:

- **[Sampling bias](/glossary#sampling-bias)** — a sample isn't representative of the population, because it over- or under-represents some part of it. Surveying the first 10 students you happen to meet, who all turn out to be women, produces a sample that says nothing reliable about the whole class.
- **[Confirmation bias](/glossary#confirmation-bias)** — searching for or interpreting information in a way that confirms what you already believed going in.
- **[Observer bias](/glossary#observer-bias)** — different people observing the same thing differently, also called experimenter bias. It's why [self-reported data](/glossary#self-reporting) is often preferable: it removes the observer from the measurement.
- **[Interpretation bias](/glossary#interpretation-bias)** — reading an ambiguous result in a positive or negative direction based on expectation rather than evidence.

## Unbiased sampling

**[Unbiased sampling](/glossary#unbiased-sampling)** means the sample actually represents the population being measured. The main lever for getting there is randomness: pulling a sample at random, rather than by convenience, gives every part of the population an equal chance of being included. Visualizing both the population and the sample side by side — the same bar chart, applied to each — is a quick way to catch a misalignment before it reaches a conclusion.

None of this is exhaustive. It's the same territory course 1 covers under [fairness](/glossary#fairness): checking who's included, whether selection was random, and whether the conclusion claims more than the data supports.
