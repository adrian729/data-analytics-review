Sharing your portfolio succeeds only if the recruiter can follow your thinking, so this module treats the interview as a translation exercise where mindset matters as much as artifacts — you establish the viewpoint of a practicing analyst and then use the case study to demonstrate it.

## Discussing your portfolio

When you discuss case studies in your portfolio with potential employers and recruiters, lead with a short elevator pitch that gives a high-level understanding before you dive into detail.

An elevator pitch is just a couple of sentences describing the idea, short enough to explain in an elevator; the Data Partners Real Estate example shows how to condense an executive summary and the business tasks that framed it — for example, "In this case study, I used real estate company data to evaluate resale performance, determine trends, and theorize what was causing them. I was then able to develop an action plan based on those findings." — then invite follow-up if they want the full recommendations.

Once the high-level context is set, give concrete examples of your process rather than reciting results or exact spreadsheet functions: if asked how you approach data cleaning, explain how you chose tools and what steps you took to make the data clean and useful, which signals how you think about cleaning in general.

The most effective framing is audience-centered: recruiters and hiring managers want your thought process and problem-solving approach, not a feature dump, so keep detail matched to whether they are technical or not and remember that practicing the pitch with a friend or trusted colleague makes the delivery natural.

## Polishing your portfolio with self-reflection

The self-reflection activity asks you to evaluate finished or in-progress pieces against what employers actually need to see, using the portfolio and case study checklist as a reference.

Ask whether anything is missing: are all pages accounted for on a website, are all projects correctly uploaded on GitHub or Kaggle, are steps in the analysis or details in descriptions absent, and is the hosting platform still the most appropriate for your goals.

Ask whether there is too much: could descriptions be tightened, is extra data included that does not add meaning or context, and would cutting it preserve the story.

Ask whether anything should not be included: references to others' work without citation should be removed or linked properly, and any extraneous or unprofessional component should go.

The reflection then makes the learning stick: identify which changes will be most significant — such as organizing to highlight the most relevant projects with clear descriptions and outcomes — and what you still plan to refine, such as adding interactivity to visualizations or continuously adding new projects as experience grows, so the portfolio remains a controlled [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders)-ready [context](/glossary#context) for every future conversation.
