Analysts who understand AI can augment routine and creative work without being replaced by it.

## What AI means for analysts

**[Generative AI](/glossary#generative-ai)** is AI that can generate new content like text, images, or other media, while AI broadly is computer programs that complete cognitive tasks typically associated with human intelligence; named examples are Gemini, ChatGPT by OpenAI, and Microsoft Copilot, with Tableau AI as an analytics example that helps prepare data, reduce repetitive tasks, and suggest visualizations.

A **[prompt](/glossary#prompt)** is input that provides instructions to an AI tool about how to generate output, typed or spoken. The unit's objectives are foundational concepts, discovery of analytics tools, and hands-on practice with a generative tool.

## Where AI tools fit in the workflow

| Category | How analysts use it |
|---|---|
| Prepare data | Collect from sources and transform into analysis-ready formats |
| Analyze data | Scan large volumes for trends, patterns, and relationships, such as summarizing customer reviews to clarify preferences |
| Automate tasks | Handle routine steps like identifying duplicates and missing fields, freeing time for stakeholder framing |

Tooling evolves quickly; evaluate available tools for your role rather than assuming one platform covers all.

## Five practical applications of generative AI

| Application | Analyst example from the sources |
|---|---|
| Create content | Generate reports, visualizations, or dashboards adjusted to the insights stakeholders need |
| Analyze information quickly | Assess campaign data and sentiment at scale |
| Answer questions in nuanced ways | Summarize research such as accessibility best practices when prompted for that nuance |
| Simplify day-to-day work | Prompt for code that fills missing values, standardizes formatting, or removes duplicates |
| Brainstorm ideas | In the Ask phase, prompt for research questions to refine a problem statement |

These are starting directions; capabilities will expand.

## Practice: data visualization with AI and prompt iteration

The optional activity casts you as a portfolio analyst using the S&P 500 to recommend adjustments with Gemini and a personal Google Account, avoiding private inputs.

Start broad — "Provide a list of visualization types for the S&P 500 over the last year and describe which fits metrics like revenue, employee count, or spending" — paste prompt and output into the template, then note one way the output fell short (generic, not finance-tuned).

Iterate by adding details, context, goal, specificity, and length — insurance quarterly review over 12 months, S&P revenue versus projected revenue, tailoring to investors buying and selling S&P 500 stocks, or requesting 25 finance visualizations — reviewing whether refinement helps until the output is useful, not perfect. The quiz reinforces prompts may be speech or text, clear instructions help, they need not be code, and context, goal, format, and length are valid refinements.

## Use AI responsibly and keep learning

Review outputs carefully, disclose AI use, and consider privacy, security, and broader effects before sharing anything stake-holder-facing; these are presented as non-negotiable checks.

For deeper study see the Data Cards Playbook, Stanford HAI, "All Things Generative AI," Global Trends 2040, GAO's spotlight, NIST on AI bias, and Google Cloud's overview, plus Google AI Essentials as the next step after Gemini — all to help you experiment with generative AI and prompting safely.
