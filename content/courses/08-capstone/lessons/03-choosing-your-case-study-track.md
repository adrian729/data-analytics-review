Choosing a track is a goal decision, not a difficulty ranking: **[Track A](/glossary#case-study-track-a)** is working with existing questions and datasets to demonstrate interview-ready job skills, while **[Track B](/glossary#case-study-track-b)** is choosing your own questions and dataset to build a flexible, interest-driven portfolio piece, and you may complete both sequentially with the same outline.

## How the tracks differ

Track A hands you a business task, dataset, and list of deliverables similar to what an employer might assign after a pre-screen — the Cyclistic bike-share packet with historical trip data to convert casual riders to members, or the Bellabeat packet with public smart-device data to guide a wellness company's marketing — so you can focus on running Ask through Share cleanly and showing process rigor. Track B asks you to originate the work: find a public dataset you care about, create the business task, decide who the audience is, and define deliverables yourself, which the course frames as the more self-directed, more personal option that showcases initiative and domain interest alongside technical execution.

## How to pick

Pick Track A if your primary goal is a recruiter-legible rehearsal for time-boxed case interviews where the question is imposed and documentation of cleaning, analysis, and recommendations is scored. Pick Track B if you want to explore housing, conservation, Bitcoin historical prices, fitness, or another passion and can shoulder scoping, sourcing, and stakeholder definition yourself; the capstone outline still guides you through each phase with key questions and activities, and you can revisit any earlier course module for support. The videos emphasize two guardrails for either choice: all data will be public and [open data](/glossary#open-data), so cite every source, and the final product must be shareable — a portfolio-ready case study rather than private interview work.

## Finding public data for Track B

Track B sources are intentionally broad: Kaggle datasets pages, government open-data portals, public repositories like Our World in Data or Johns Hopkins COVID-19 series, and curated example collections on Medium, GitHub, and Tableau Public. Evaluate any candidate on fit to a definable [population](/glossary#population) versus the [sample](/glossary#sample) you actually receive, on licensing and citation terms, and on whether the data can support a clear business question through the full data analysis process; a dataset that cannot plausibly answer a stakeholder's decision is a poor capstone vehicle even if the topic feels interesting.
