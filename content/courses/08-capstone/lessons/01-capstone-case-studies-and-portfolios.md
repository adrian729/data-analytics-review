A **[capstone project](/glossary#capstone-project)** is the end-of-certificate project that Coursera calls the capstone — in the program it is the optional but highly recommended final deliverable that brings Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyze, Share, and Act together on a real scenario — and in hiring outside the classroom employers call the same work a **[case study](/glossary#case-study)**, a practice data-analytics project given with a dataset and business question, often under a 24-to-48-hour limit, to assess how you approach a problem rather than whether every answer is perfect.

## What each artifact is

The course defines the trio tightly: the capstone is the curricular container, the case study is the documented walkthrough inside it, and a **[portfolio](/glossary#portfolio)** is the curated collection of case studies you host publicly and link from your resume. The introductory video illustrates the case study shape with a predicting-employee-attrition example — title and industry focus, problem statement and specific goals, deliverables such as a presentation with findings and recommendations, and the downloadable dataset that anchors the work — while the portfolio is not a single analysis but the body of examples, hosted on GitHub, Kaggle, Tableau, or a personal blog, that lets a recruiter see repeated evidence of how you work.

## Why employers value them

Rishie, Global Analytics Skills Curriculum Manager, reframes what hiring managers score: data is fundamental across industries, so the analyst is not just a technical executor but an artist whose creativity and storytelling decide if insight is remembered. Employers do not expect you to know every right answer; they listen for how you think, why you chose that angle, and whether you can challenge traditional norms to tell the story the data holds. That is why the course repeats the instruction to capture process over results — describe strategies, tools, decisions and assumptions — and to show background, metrics definitions, and documentation of SQL queries or spreadsheets so the reader can follow the logic from question to recommendation.

## Introducing yourself for the capstone

The discussion prompt operationalizes the point: after seven courses you write a 100–150-word introduction and a 150–200-word reflection on why employers would value your portfolio, then respond to at least two peers. The activity is low-stakes on correctness and high-stakes on visibility — it surfaces career identity, invites feedback on narrative clarity, and rehearses the same move the portfolio later makes at scale: telling who you are, what you chose to emphasize, and why that mix of skills maps to the work you want next, with links to your hosted portfolio as the proof.
