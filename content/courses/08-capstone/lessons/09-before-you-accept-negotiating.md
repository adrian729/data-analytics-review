An offer is the start of a negotiation, not the end of the interview — the module urges resisting the impulse to accept on the spot and instead reviewing every detail of the contract.

## Research and look beyond salary

Research the role's average salary for a junior data analyst in your location before you name a number; understand what distinguishes you and ask early what range the team is targeting and which components are flexible — sign-on bonuses, equity, merit increases, vacation, paid time off, sick days, retirement, healthcare — so you evaluate total compensation and growth potential, not just base pay.

If you are asked for a salary expectation at the start, avoid anchoring with a specific number before you know the budgeted range, because naming first cedes leverage; wait for the written offer that outlines the package and then respond with a well-researched counter-offer that demonstrates you know your value.

The reading emphasizes that negotiating is expected and not impolite: a confident, evidence-based request signals tenacity and judgment, and if the counter is not met and no competing offer exists, accepting a fair initial package with a plan to reevaluate after about twelve months preserves long-term growth.

## Scenario: negotiating terms

The negotiating-terms video models this progression.

Jordan offers Sally the junior data analyst position at $50,000 plus healthcare, stock options, and paid time off; Sally thanks him and asks for time to review, which Jordan grants with comprehensive materials.

In the follow-up, Sally counters with $58,000 to $60,000, citing transferable communication strengths from managing customers and classrooms, ability to translate complex topics for non-quantitative [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders), and technical tools from the Certificate including SQL and data visualization; Jordan notes the ask is above the local average and invites justification, then meets in the middle at $56,000 with room for growth, which Sally accepts pending paperwork.

Key behaviors highlighted are requesting review time, grounding the range in specific soft and hard skills, and accepting a collaborative compromise without pressure.

## Career perspective — Nathan and VetNet

Nathan, principal data analyst in trust and safety at Google and member of VetNet, an employee resource group for veterans, describes an annual resume review workshop helping veterans and spouses remove military jargon, claim appropriate credit for accomplishments, and measure impact so contributions jump off the page.

He connects analyst success to the same fundamentals veterans practice: clear communication, working well with cross-functional [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) in collaborative settings, and attention to detail that protects credibility, noting that the best analysis is worthless if recommendations cannot be communicated and adopted.

Advice for entering analysts emphasizes continuing training through the Certificate and tool-specific courses, pursuing problems you care about with self-directed data, and valuing persistence and humility — veterans' persistent yet humble mindset prevents ego-driven blind spots — captured in the maxim that it is better to get speeding tickets than parking tickets: jump into unfamiliar situations, learn, and collaborate your way out.

Collectively these lessons frame negotiation and career entry as stakeholder-sensitive communication where research, justified advocacy, and willingness to learn outweigh simply naming a number.
