The interview tests whether you can do the work and whether colleagues want to work with you — each round evaluates technical fit and professionalism.

## The four stages

| Stage | Who and purpose | What to bring and expect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Introduction | Recruiter screens resume and portfolio; get to know you, background, and fit | Concise highlights of qualifications, experience, and skills with specific examples; be ready to ask about company and role |
| 2 — Skill test | Fellow analyst or data engineer; SQL, programming, and case study or behavioral exercise | Well-formed answers that demonstrate technical knowledge and problem-solving; may receive a take-home case to present |
| 3 — Compatibility | Wider team to gauge culture and collaboration | Examples from portfolio and resume that show how you work with peers and managers |
| 4 — Decision | You ask next steps and timeline (often four to six weeks) | Expect offer, rejection, or silence; if rejected, send a brief thank-you to preserve the relationship; if silent, follow up professionally |

Persistence across stages is framed as a career skill.

## Scenario videos — what to model

Introductions: Sally, from teaching and retail via the Certificate to a junior analyst at BWR Technical Services, answers "tell me about yourself," owning a teacher-budgeting initiative, translating classroom and customer skills, and asking about stand-ups and datasets — Jordan assigns a case study.

Case study: "Bridging the Communication Gap between Teachers & Parents" for Creekside Middle School — app for sporadic communication; Why goal to quantify involvement impact on scores, How goal to learn parent interests; findings of falling scores since 2004 as involvement dropped despite a 14-point lift when involved, with seventh graders getting fewest alerts; recommendation of three tabs and a seventh-grade pilot; timeline of two weeks analysis then build and launch.

Problem-solving: retail bike-clip complaint — assess the problem, rule out shoe size, find incompatible clips, escalate to a recall, offer refund or voucher; method is empathy for [context](/glossary#context) and root-cause mapping.

## What makes a great pitch and how to prepare

An **[elevator pitch](/glossary#elevator-pitch)** is a short statement describing an idea in a couple of sentences, short enough to deliver in an elevator; the module ties it directly to the executive summary of your case study and urges condensing that summary rather than reciting full analysis.

For "tell me about yourself," questions cluster into technical — tools (SQL, Excel, R), integrity via validation, SQL roles — and personal — initiative, hardest project, explaining a complex topic to [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders), adapting when plans change — draft and rehearse.

Four tips: map keywords between listing and resume; use I accomplished X as measured by Y doing Z (e.g., increased satisfaction 22% in three months by designing onboarding) or strong verbs; stock "tell me about a time when ..." stories; arrive with questions about projects, goals, and team.

## Practice with Interview Warmup

**[Interview Warmup](/glossary#interview-warmup)** is a Google tool at grow.google/interview-warmup; select Data Analytics twice for a ten-minute session with two background, one behavioral, and two technical questions, repeating as often as desired.

It transcribes answers and provides insights: talking points with gaps to cover, most-used words with synonyms, and job-related terms with a full list — reviewed privately, not graded — to build confidence before live [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) conversations.
