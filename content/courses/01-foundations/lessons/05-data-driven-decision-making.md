[Data-driven decision-making](/glossary#data-driven-decision-making) is using facts to guide business strategy, rather than relying on assumption or observation alone. A data-driven decision sees the whole picture; one made on observation or gut feeling alone sees only part of it.

## Data is not the whole answer

The nuance the course insists on: **data alone is not the whole picture either.** Combining data with human and subject-matter expertise produces better decisions than either on its own.

[Gut instinct](/glossary#gut-instinct) is an intuitive judgment made with little or no explicit reasoning. It cuts both ways:

- It **introduces bias** and leads to costly mistakes when used without data to back it up.
- It also **surfaces patterns data alone might miss**, because domain experience encodes things that were never recorded.

The right blend depends on the project. A rushed project with little data leans harder on experience; a well-resourced one should lean harder on the data. Neither extreme is the goal.

## The business task

An [issue](/glossary#business-task) is a topic to investigate. A **question** is designed to discover information. A **problem** is an obstacle that needs resolving. The [business task](/glossary#business-task) is the specific question or problem that data analysis resolves for a business — the concrete unit of work an analyst is actually assigned.

Example: a zoo with a staffing problem doesn't hand an analyst "fix staffing." The business task is *"analyze weather data from the last decade to identify predictable attendance patterns."*

## What this looks like in practice

The course's illustrations are worth keeping because they show how varied the shape of a business task can be:

| Organization | Data used | Decision it drove |
|---|---|---|
| Vending machine operator | Flavor-combination data from AI-equipped machines | Which new products to develop |
| Search engine | Reliability and accuracy signals | How to rank results |
| Zoo | A decade of historical weather data | Staffing levels against predicted attendance |
| Hospital | Foot-traffic data | Shift staffing, to cut patient wait times |

In every one, the analyst's contribution is not the decision — it is making the decision answerable with facts.
