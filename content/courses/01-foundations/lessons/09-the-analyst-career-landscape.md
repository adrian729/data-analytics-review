The same underlying data produces completely different jobs depending on who holds it. The same company revenue figures might be used by a bank's analyst to forecast stock price, by that company's own business analyst to advise its sales team, and by a software vendor's analyst to measure how well their product performs for that client. Same data, three unrelated roles.

## Titles that sound alike

| Title | What it actually does |
|---|---|
| Business analyst | Analyzes data to help businesses improve processes, products, or services |
| Data analytics consultant | Analyzes the systems and models an organization uses for data |
| Data engineer | Prepares and integrates data from different sources for analytical use |
| Data scientist | Uses expert skills in technology and social science to find trends through data analysis |
| Data specialist | Organizes or converts data for use in databases or software systems |
| Operations analyst | Analyzes data to assess the performance of business operations and workflows |

Companies routinely blur these lines in job postings, so **the listed skills and responsibilities matter more than the title.**

## The three-way distinction

The clearest split, and the one worth carrying into interviews:

| | Data analyst | Data scientist | Data specialist |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem-solving** | Uses existing tools and methods on existing data types | Invents new tools and models, asks open-ended questions, collects new data types | Uses in-depth database knowledge to solve problems |
| **Analysis focus** | Analyzes collected data to help stakeholders decide | Analyzes and interprets complex data to make business predictions | Organizes large data volumes for analytics and operations |
| **Other skills** | Database queries, visualization, dashboards, reports, spreadsheets | Advanced statistics, machine learning, deep learning, data optimization, programming | Data manipulation, information security, data models, scalability, disaster recovery |

Industry-specialist variants — business, marketing, HR/payroll, financial, risk, healthcare analyst — are the same core skill set applied to one domain's data and questions.

## Choosing a direction

Four factors the course names for weighing a role:

- **Industry** — each has different data needs and demands different skills.
- **Personal interest** — a domain that means something to you strengthens both motivation and your interview answers.
- **Location and travel** — commute, remote preference, willingness to relocate.
- **Culture fit** — team versus solo, routine versus novel work, values alignment.

## Market and interview notes

An IBM-backed study using Burning Glass data (February 2021 – January 2022) estimated **over 380,000 open data analytics postings in the US** — and most of them don't literally say "data analyst" in the title, which is why searching by title alone under-counts the market badly.

For interviews:

- Have a **concrete story** ready about using data to solve a real problem.
- Build an **active professional presence** — LinkedIn, and a GitHub with visible project work. Recruiters search these platforms directly.
- Prepare **specific, informed questions** for the interviewer rather than generic ones.
- In case-study interviews, the interviewer is usually evaluating your **reasoning process** on a sample dataset, not scoring a single correct answer.
