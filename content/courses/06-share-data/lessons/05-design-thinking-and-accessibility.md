A polished chart that the intended viewers cannot perceive or understand is not a successful visualization, so the module pairs a creative process with practical accessibility checks and a timeboxed workflow that lets an analyst test those checks before presenting. All three draw from the same premise: the data cannot explain itself, and as a visualization moves further from its maker it must stand alone without verbal rescue, so decisions about color, labeling, and interactivity have to be made from the viewer's point of view rather than the analyst's preference.

## A user-centric cycle: design thinking

**[Design thinking](/glossary#design-thinking)** is a process used to solve complex problems in a user-centric way, and the course presents it in five phases that need not follow a set order. Empathize means thinking about the emotions and needs of the target audience for the [data visualization](/glossary#data-visualization) — whether [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders), team members, or the general public — and noticing obstacles; the pharmaceutical example asks whether a favored palette is too bright for serious data, lacks contrast for color vision deficiencies, or needs a verbal alternative for a vision-impaired teammate. Define means figuring out exactly what that audience needs from the data; for the banking donut chart of monthly spending this surfaces goals beyond saving money, such as tracking income or discretionary spending and paying off debt. Ideate means generating visualization ideas — drafting color, shape, or chart variants filtered through audience needs. Prototype means putting visualizations together for feedback, and test means showing them to teammates before [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) see them and iterating on critiques.

## Highlighting what matters with pre-attentive attributes

**[Pre-attentive attributes](/glossary#pre-attentive-attributes)** are the elements of a [data visualization](/glossary#data-visualization) that an audience recognizes automatically without conscious effort, and the pro-tips reading shows how headlines, subtitles, clear labeling, and annotations direct that automatic attention. The reading frames this on two building blocks: a **[mark](/glossary#mark)** is a visual object such as a point, line, or shape, carrying the qualities of position, size, shape, and color, while a **[channel](/glossary#channel)** is a visual aspect or variable that represents characteristics of the data, judged by how accurately it encodes values, how strongly it pops out, and how well it groups. The guideline the sources tie to them is the five-second test: within five seconds a viewer should know what they are observing, and within the next five understand the conclusion. Using color and value contrast to emphasize the focal insight, labeling data directly instead of forcing a legend lookup, and placing annotation immediately next to the point it explains are all ways of exploiting pre-attentive processing so the viewer does not have to hunt for the takeaway.

## Making that emphasis accessible to everyone

Accessibility means ensuring graphs, charts, and tables can be interacted with by anyone, including people with long-term or temporary auditory, visual, or sensory impairments — over one billion people worldwide live with a disability. The most actionable fixes also speed reading for viewers without disabilities: label data directly instead of relying exclusively on legends, provide **[alternative text](/glossary#alternative-text)** — text that provides an alternative to non-text content such as images and videos — so content can become large print, braille, or speech, and make data available as a text export to Sheets or Excel. Separate foreground from background with high-contrast colors, distinguish with texture and shape rather than color alone (avoid red on green or green on red, and blue on green or yellow on red), and keep visuals simple by breaking complex data into smaller views instead of crowding one view.

## From idea to testable chart in 60 minutes

To move from intention to evidence quickly, the reading timeboxes chart creation into 60 minutes: prep for five minutes to create mental and physical space and consider amount and type of data; talk and listen for fifteen minutes to get to the "ask behind the ask" and set expectations with stakeholder feedback; sketch and design for twenty minutes to draft the approach and define timing and output; and prototype and improve for twenty minutes to generate the visual, gauge its effectiveness, and repeat until ready. Analysts will develop their own version with experience, but the routine preserves the same user-centric check — what the audience perceives — before publication.
