Sharing a narrative succeeds only if the audience can follow it and stay focused, so the module treats presentation as the delivery vehicle for the story rather than a repeat of the spreadsheet — a logically organized, interesting deck that makes key messages clear and earns confidence, while a messy set of images erodes trust.

## The five parts of an effective narrative

An effective narrative shared with [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) needs characters, setting, plot, big reveal, and aha moment. Characters are the people affected — [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) themselves, customers, or clients — and naming them adds human context for why they care. Setting describes what is happening, how often, what tasks are involved, and other background about the current situation. Plot, sometimes called conflict, creates tension — a competitor challenge, an inefficient process, or a new opportunity — and reveals the problem the analysis must solve so characters are compelled to act. The big reveal, or resolution, shows how the data solves that problem, and the aha moment shares recommendations and explains why they will help the company succeed; together these turn insights into a clear story tied to the [business task](/glossary#business-task).

## Presentation choices that keep attention

Visuals must support the narrative quickly and without ambiguity: choose themes to control color, font, formatting, and positioning for a consistent look, include a title, subtitle, and date created or last updated — especially when data may change — and keep text to fewer than five lines and 25 words per slide so the audience listens instead of reading. Avoid slang, unknown abbreviations, and region-specific phrases, limit each slide to one or two visuals that directly support the key message, and direct the eye with arrows, callouts, or labels. How a visual is added matters: copying and pasting lets you edit it directly in the deck but it will not update when the source changes, linking keeps a live connection via URL so edits appear automatically and is the method to use when data is likely to change, embedding creates an independent copy that does not auto-update, and screenshotting is the snapshot fallback used when dashboard images are saved as pictures.

## Practicing, sharing, and managing imposter syndrome

A light practice loop helps the story land: create screenshots of the dashboard, build a short deck for stakeholders curious but not close to the project — title slide, one or two screenshots per slide with brief context, closing takeaways — and record a two-to-three minute delivery to check for an attention-grabbing opening, broad ideas before specifics, short sentences, intentional five-second pauses after each visualization, level pitch, purposeful movement, good posture, and eye contact. For the slide deck check for a descriptive title, visible date, readable font size, named metrics, and effective charts. Even with preparation, sharing can trigger imposter syndrome, a career theme Sundas addresses as analytical lead at Google: as the first in her family to graduate university, an immigrant who began after a six-year gap at a community college while raising children, she recalls fearing exposure with senior leaders and advising networking with people who share the same background, pep talks, and a log of successes and failures — so the story is shared not despite nerves but through them, with the [report](/glossary#report) reflecting the journey.
