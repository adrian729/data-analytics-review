The Share phase of the [data analysis process](/glossary#data-analysis-process) only works if the audience can follow it, so the module frames presentation as art and science: science is consistent structure, art is tailoring it to this audience.

A **[framework](/glossary#framework)** is the context a presentation needs to create logical connections that tie back to the business task and metrics — set this context before any chart appears.

## Framework that ties back to the business task

The framework starts from the [business task](/glossary#business-task) — the question your analysis answers — and filters what makes the deck.

In the avocado example the task is tracking avocado searches to plan stocking, so the opening states the task and lists goals: historical overview, seasonal trends, and next steps.

[Metrics](/glossary#metric) are then made explicit — "avocado" queries from 2004 to 2018, United States only, as Google Trends scores normalized to 100 — so source, dates, coverage, and scale are clear.

That task-plus-metrics setup supplies the [context](/glossary#context) the audience needs to turn numbers into decisions.

Speaker notes hold those points and can be shared ahead for accessibility.

## Weaving data into the deck

Weaving data in starts by answering four questions: what data was available, whether new relevant data appeared, whether different data would be needed for a narrower question, and what the hypothesis is.

The hypothesis is the theory you try to prove or disprove; stating early that average monthly price trends should reveal coming demand gives the audience a lens.

The McCandless Method moves general to specific in five steps: introduce the graphic by name, answer when/where/how questions, state the shared insight, call out supporting data, and explain why it matters.

Framing first ("this circular time plot shows peak months so we can anticipate demand") and details after ("the week of November 25, 2018 scored around 49 versus 90 the week of February 4") model that progression.

Ask of every chart: does this support the takeaway you want people to carry away — if not, remove it.

## Critique, messy versus good

The 13-point reading turns those choices into criteria: title, subtitle, and last-updated date; logical sequence; agenda with timing; text scannable in five seconds; the [business task](/glossary#business-task) up front; hypothesis; the [business metrics](/glossary#metric) used; visualizations with titles introduced by name; general-to-specific flow; speaker notes; and clear takeaways.

Track them in an evaluation table with columns for slide number, what worked well, and what could be improved.

The messy health-and-happiness deck violates most of them: long generic title over a stock image, no purpose statement, no titles on data slides, multiple scatterplots at once with dense text, inconsistent placement and no theme, and a "Laughter is the best medicine" conclusion with no flow — so people read instead of listening.

The good version simplifies the title slide with presenter and date, adds a purpose statement and table of contents with transition slides, and introduces each visual first without text.

It then directs gaze with animation and annotation (the number inside each country is its happiness score, bluer is higher), explains correlation plainly (about 0.50 for health versus happiness, 0.70 for GDP versus health), and notes caveats, then ends with logical conclusions — wealth, health, and happiness move together without implying causation — plus an appendix, showing [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) that flow, theme, and limits let an audience grasp facts, meaning, and next steps.
