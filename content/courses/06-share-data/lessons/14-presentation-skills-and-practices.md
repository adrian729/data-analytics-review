A data analyst has two jobs — analyze data and present findings so people can act — and delivery decides whether the second job succeeds.

| Moment | Do this | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Breathe deeply to channel adrenaline; rehearse by dress rehearsal, script, or visualization | Turns nervous energy into focus and rewards preparation |
| Open | Start broad — context and obvious questions — then go specific; introduce each graphic by name | General-to-specific orients the audience first |
| Chart | Wait 5 seconds, ask if it is clear, explain if needed, wait 5 more seconds, then give the conclusion | Gives first-time viewers time to process |
| Every slide | Write speaker notes and keep on-slide text scannable in 5 seconds | Notes keep flow; concise slides let people listen |

## Guide: share findings

Know your flow by asking who the audience is and what the purpose is.

Executives want high-level results and recommendations with little process; managers expect more analysis detail and data questions; analysts welcome deep dives.

If the purpose is to request or recommend, build toward recommendations; if to focus on results, mark the path with breadcrumbs; if to provide a report, let key findings speak clearly.

Prepare talking points and limit text: keep each block readable in five seconds, do not duplicate notes on the slide, and use animation to reveal one bullet or visual at a time.

End with recommendations on one slide: be clear, give actionable next steps and what success looks like — the mountaintop after the climb.

Allow time: keep the deck as short as the story allows, watch timing per slide, ensure each slide carries a unique part of the narrative, and reserve minutes for questions.

Use the sample arc: agenda with timed bullets (introductions 4, overview 5, data 10, recommendations 3, actions 3, questions 5), purpose slide, one or two data slides with verbal cues, a recommendations slide, and a call to action; keep extra visuals in an appendix.

## Evaluate your own deck

Treat each rehearsal as dialogue, not monologue.

Ask: do I open with attention, move broad to specific, speak in short sentences, pause five seconds after each visual, keep pitch level, stand still and move with purpose, hold good posture, and look at the audience or camera?

For the deck: does each slide have a clear title and subtitle, a last-updated date, a readable font size, named business metrics, and effective visuals with titles and legends?

## Present like a pro

Plan for accessibility — share the deck by email and check that visuals are understandable to everyone — and remember the curse of knowledge: steps feel obvious to you but not to listeners.

Answer basic questions early: where the data came from and how it was collected, what time and place it covers, the guiding hypothesis and goals, and any assumptions such as grouping months by season.

Stay focused: avoid side stories and detail that does not concern this audience, and strip charts to only what advances discussion.

Deliver with care: keep sentences and words short, build intentional pauses, keep pitch level, notice habits such as speeding up or fidgeting, practice stillness, and make positive eye contact. Seek feedback from people you trust after every talk.
