**[Data storytelling](/glossary#data-storytelling)** is communicating the meaning of a dataset with visuals and a narrative that are customized for a particular audience, and it succeeds where raw results do not because stories are the oldest form of teaching — human brains organize events with a beginning, middle, and end, which helps people process, remember, and care, a point Stephen Few captures as numbers having a story to tell that relies on the analyst to give it a clear and convincing voice.

## The three steps that make a story stick

The module teaches three steps — engage your audience, create compelling visuals that show rather than just tell, and tell the story in an interesting narrative — and the visuals carry the story by taking the audience on a journey of how data changed over time or what it means, so a pie chart of profit by store or a word cloud sized by frequency quickly reveals what a spreadsheet column cannot, while a **[story](/glossary#story)** is that narrative itself, the sequenced account with a beginning, middle, and end that connects insights to the project objective in concise, organized form. The Ben Wellington New York noise-complaints tour shows the same pattern tuned for readers: define context first with a combo table and bar chart of complaint categories, analyze variables next with a stacked area chart by hour that passes the five-second rule, then draw conclusions with density and neighborhood maps where a dark-blue noisy block beside a white quiet one is instantly legible.

## Engaging your audience and spotlighting what matters

All successful storytelling starts with who is listening, which the course frames as **[engagement](/glossary#engagement)** — capturing and holding someone's interest and attention during a data presentation — and analysts build it by asking what role the audience plays, what is their stake, and what they hope to get, since a printing-company stakeholder worries about paper and staffing for a monthly cycle while authors and editors worry about writing faster. When many insights compete, **[spotlighting](/glossary#spotlighting)** is scanning through data to quickly identify the most important insights: write each insight on paper or a sticky note, spread them on a whiteboard as an archaeologist would artifacts, and look for broad ideas that keep recurring and patterns repeating until a clear, concise key message emerges.

## A data-journalism model

Data journalists face the same job as analysts — do not just link to a database, but uncover the meaning beneath it and give it a point of view — and the course holds up Wellington's use of New York City's open data portal alongside Carolyn's path from data journalist at the Chicago Tribune to measurement lead at Google. The Wellington example shows to pair tables with charts, align legends with chart colors, and check that percentages add to 100 percent while noting a pie with 11 categories still misleads. Carolyn's lesson, from John Snow mapping cholera cases to one London pump in the 1850s to her own doodled charts, is to know your tools — Tableau, Data Studio, Excel — but never let the tool overwhelm the story; the audience wants what you distilled and why it matters.
