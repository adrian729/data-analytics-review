You can do every earlier phase right and still fail at Share if the audience cannot see what the data means. The [data analysis process](/glossary#data-analysis-process) ends with Share then Act, and Share is where analysis becomes useful: you translate results into a form [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders) can absorb in seconds, and [data visualization](/glossary#data-visualization) is the graphic representation and presentation of that data — putting information into an image to make it easier to understand. Stakeholders often lack the time, access, or expertise to find the story themselves, so the visualization must do that work.

## Why visualizing brings data to life

Maps organized geographic data as early as 1502 and bar graphs looked recognizably modern by 1821, yet the digital age since the 1990s expanded both the scope of data and the reach of visuals. The sources frame effective work with two time tests — know what you are looking at within five seconds, grasp the conclusion within the next five — and with David McCandless's four elements: information (the data), story (the narrative), goal (the function), and visual form (the structure and beauty). Analysts split time both ways — reading visuals to draw conclusions and creating visuals to tell a story — and in either direction the visualization is the key to helping people without the same background form their own opinions.

## Anatomy of an effective visualization

An effective visualization communicates meaning without overwhelming, and its labeled parts each do one job. A **[Headline](/glossary#headline)** is text at the top of a visualization that communicates the data being presented, while a **[Subtitle](/glossary#subtitle)** supports that headline by adding context and description. A **[Label](/glossary#label)** identifies a value or describes a scale, and a **[Legend](/glossary#legend)** identifies the meaning of various elements so color or pattern is not a guess. An **[Annotation](/glossary#annotation)** briefly explains data or helps focus the audience on a particular aspect of the data, calling attention to the insight rather than leaving the viewer to hunt for it. Together these elements let a viewer who was not in the analysis reach the same conclusion more quickly.

## Static versus dynamic

Whether a visualization should be interactive is a deliberate choice about control and audience. A **[Static visualization](/glossary#static-visualization)** does not change over time unless it is edited — any visualization printed on paper is automatically static, and charts in spreadsheets are often static because the owner must change the underlying data to update. **[Dynamic visualizations](/glossary#dynamic-visualizations)** are interactive or change over time: users control what they see, and some update automatically by the minute, day, or week. The Tableau example makes the tradeoff concrete: a dashboard tracks happiness scores 2015–2017 with countries ranked by change on the left and a map colored blue (highest) to red (lowest) on the right, plus a year slider set to 2016. That interactivity helps stakeholders explore, but the more power you give the user, the less control you have over the story — so the choice depends on data, audience, and presentation.
