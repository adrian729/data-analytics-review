Before solving a problem, understand it — that's what [structured thinking](/glossary#structured-thinking) is for. A [scope of work](/glossary#scope-of-work) makes that understanding concrete: it's the agreed-upon outline of the tasks to be performed during a project, written down before work starts so everyone — you and your stakeholders — agrees on what's in and out of scope.

Scope matters because it defines the [problem domain](/glossary#problem-domain): every activity that affects, or is affected by, the problem you're solving. A vague scope lets the problem domain quietly expand mid-project — new requests get added without anyone renegotiating the timeline.

## Staying objective

Data doesn't mean much without context, and context is personal — two analysts can curate the same dataset, follow the same steps, and still reach different conclusions, because conscious and subconscious biases (cultural, social, market) shape how each of them interprets it. Staying objective means recognizing all sides of an argument before drawing a conclusion, not just the one your own bias points to.

To put a dataset into context, ask:

- **Who** collected it, and **why**? Data is sometimes collected — or even fabricated — to serve an agenda.
- **What** does it represent, and how does it relate to other data?
- **When** was it collected? Older data can carry limitations a present-day situation no longer matches.
- **Where** and **how** was it collected? A survey and an in-person interview of the same population can produce different results.

If the analysis isn't objective, the conclusions can be misleading — a real limitation of data analytics. The best defense is starting with an accurate representation of the population and collecting the data in the most appropriate, objective way available.

A scope of work and an objective read of the data are the two things that keep a project's problem domain from drifting out from under you.
