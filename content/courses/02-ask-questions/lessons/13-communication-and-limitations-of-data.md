## Know your audience before you write

Before drafting an update, answer four questions:

1. Who is your audience?
2. What do they already know?
3. What do they need to know?
4. How can you best communicate what they need to know?

An update built around those four answers reads as considerate rather than generic — it tells the reader exactly what changed and what to do next, instead of everything you know about the project.

## Data has limits — know them before you trust it

**Incomplete or nonexistent data.** You might discover mid-analysis that you don't have enough data to reach a conclusion — or that you're answering a different question than the one asked. Be upfront about what's missing rather than quietly working around it.

**Misaligned data.** Different teams often measure the same thing differently — one team counts everyone who registered for a program, another counts only people who finished it. Agree on definitions early so comparisons stay meaningful.

**Dirty data.** Errors, duplicates, and inconsistent formatting cause productivity loss and bad decisions. Cleaning data — and tracking what you changed — is what prevents that.

**Small sample size.** A few unusual responses can skew a small sample's results. If the data is too thin to draw a conclusion, say so, and look for ways to collect more before forming a judgment.

Avinash Kaushik's data-storytelling advice (from his blog *Occam's Razor*) pairs well with these limits: compare like with like, set the Y-axis to zero so small changes don't look dramatic, use a plain table instead of a chart when a table tells the story just as clearly, and test for statistical significance before treating a difference as real.
