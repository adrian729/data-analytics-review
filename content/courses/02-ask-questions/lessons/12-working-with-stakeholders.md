Every project has [stakeholders](/glossary#stakeholders), and they usually fall into three groups.

**Executive team** — VPs, C-suite, senior leadership. They think at a high level, want headline results first, and have very little time. Lead your presentations with answers; keep detail in an appendix. Your project manager is your bridge to this group — they'll get you approvals and feedback more often than the executives will speak to you directly.

**Customer-facing team** — anyone with regular customer contact, compiling feedback and setting expectations. They often arrive with a specific ask ("find X pattern"). Let the data tell the story rather than confirming what they hope to find.

**Data science team** — other analysts, data scientists, data engineers you collaborate with directly. Different people investigate different angles of the same problem (e.g. one analyst looks at productivity data, another at hiring data) and share findings so a data scientist can combine them into a bigger picture.

## Working effectively with each group

- **Discuss goals.** A request is usually tied to a bigger goal — ask about it before diving in.
- **Feel empowered to say no.** If a deadline is unrealistic or a hypothesis isn't well-formed, push back and explain why. [Reframing](/glossary#reframing) the request — restating it, then redirecting toward what's actually achievable — is usually more useful than a flat refusal.
- **Plan for the unexpected.** List potential roadblocks before starting, and build slack into the timeline.
- **Know your project.** Track discussions over email or reports so you can answer "why does this matter" questions at any point.
- **Start with words and visuals together.** People absorb information differently; pairing a description with a quick visual heads off the "illusion of agreement" that sends projects back and forth before a direction is settled.
- **Communicate often.** Regular updates — project milestones, setbacks, changes — plus a change-log stakeholders can check on their own, prevent surprises.

*Example: a data science team working to reduce employee [turnover rate](/glossary#turnover-rate) might split the analysis — one analyst on productivity data, another on hiring data — then hand both to a data scientist who predicts how process changes would affect engagement.*
